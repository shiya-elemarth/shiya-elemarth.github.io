Fig Wasps, Ocean Winds, and Learning to Navigate with AI

2026-11-18

Recently, my manager shared a metaphor to encourage us to embrace AI: think of bees or wasps migrating across an ocean, where some adapt to the journey far better than others.

The image that stuck with me most is the fig wasp. On its own, a tiny fig wasp could never fly across vast stretches of open ocean. To reach distant islands, it relies on wind currents to carry it across distances that would otherwise be impossible—taking on enormous risk in search of a specific host fig tree on a remote shore.

Lately, I feel very much like that wasp, and AI feels like the wind.

Looking at how much AI has progressed just from 2025 to 2026, things that once felt out of reach are suddenly possible. In fact, this website itself is a small example: I am not a software engineer or web developer, yet with AI helping me piece together the code and structure, I was able to build and launch a personal blog from scratch. At a much larger scale, entrepreneur and investor Dinesh Moorjani recently noted in a keynote speech how AI is dramatically lowering barriers—making it possible to sit at home and build products or companies without a traditional engineering team, and unlocking breakthroughs that used to be unimaginable.

And yet, being carried by a strong wind doesn't automatically tell you where to land.

Even in the long term, it isn't 100% clear what my "host fig tree" looks like—or what those remote islands will even look like a year from now. When the pace of change is this fast, it is easy to feel overwhelmed and lost. It raises questions I don't have neat answers to yet: What does it actually mean to be "best adapted"? How do you navigate when the destination keeps shifting? And does moving faster always mean moving better?

One reflection from Moorjani's talk helped ground me a bit. He drew a clear line between what AI accelerates and what still belongs to humans. AI can process abundance of information and optimize existing paths, he argued, but it doesn't inherently understand the human condition—like how Tinder's double-blind matching wasn't just an algorithmic tweak, but an answer to a deeply human fear of rejection. As he put it, when tools make building easier, the real challenge becomes deciding what is worth your attention: "You have to be thoughtful about what you focus on, what you want to build. And what problems are you trying to solve?"

Maybe being "best adapted" isn't about riding the fastest gust of wind or trying every new tool the moment it launches. Maybe it is simply learning how to stay steady in the air, keeping our human curiosity and judgment intact, and paying close attention to which problems genuinely matter to us.

I don't have the full map yet, and that is okay. Part of why I started this space is to have an honest place to document the struggle, the small experiments, and the process of figuring it out along the way.
